You may have to choose in the middle of an emergency. These questions take two minutes and help you avoid the usual traps.

Choose before the emergency if you can.

Water damage rarely arrives at a convenient time, so the best moment to pick a company is before you need one. If that is not possible, use the minutes after you stop the water to make two or three calls and compare how they answer. A company that answers questions clearly on the phone tends to explain the work clearly on site.

Do not be pressured into signing a large contract before the crew has seen the damage. A reasonable company explains scope and price first.

Questions to ask on the phone.

Write down the answers.

  • Do you answer 24 hours a day? Water does not wait for office hours..
  • How do you decide what to dry and what to remove? The answer should mention moisture meters..
  • What standard do you follow? Look for the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard for water damage restoration..
  • Will you explain scope and price before starting? The answer should be yes..
  • Do you document the loss? Photos, readings and an itemized scope help your insurer..
  • Are you insured? Ask for proof if you want it..

Judge by behavior, not by advertising.

What a good phone call sounds like.

A good dispatcher asks basic questions before talking about price. Where is the water coming from, how deep is it, what is the floor made of, is anyone in danger and is the power on? Those questions tell the crew what equipment to bring and which hazards to expect.

They will also tell you what to do while you wait. Expect advice to shut off the water, lift belongings and stay clear of electrical risks. If a company moves straight to a sales pitch or asks for payment details before knowing the situation, treat that as a warning sign.

Insurance and direct billing.

Some companies say they bill insurers directly. That arrangement can work but you remain responsible for understanding your policy and the invoice. Ask what you will owe if the insurer pays less than the bill. Some companies will not bill insurers at all and give you the documents to submit.

Be careful about signing an assignment of benefits, which lets the company deal directly with your insurer and may limit your control over the claim. Read what you sign, and ask for a copy.

What a good written scope contains.

A scope lists the rooms affected, the work planned in each, the equipment to be placed and the expected duration of drying. It separates emergency mitigation from repairs and states how readings will be recorded. It also says what happens if the crew finds more damage once walls are opened.

You should be able to read it and understand what you are agreeing to. See structural drying and water extraction for what those line items mean.

Check licenses where they apply.

General water extraction and drying do not carry the same state licensing as mold work. If mold is present, New York requires licensed mold assessors and remediators for projects above a threshold, and the same company cannot do both on one project. Verify any license with the New York State Department of Labor.

Plumbing and electrical work require licensed tradespeople under local rules. Ask who will handle each part, and read the mold remediation page if growth is suspected.

After the crew leaves.

Ask for a final package: the moisture log, photos, the list of removed materials and the invoice. Keep these with your policy. If a mold or odor problem appears later, those records show what was done and when.

Follow up with a call if anything seems off. A good company wants to know and will explain or return. If the problem is outside their work, such as a plumbing leak, they should say so.

Documents worth asking for.

Keep copies of everything you sign.

The written scope.

It should list rooms, work, equipment and the planned drying period. Ask how changes are approved.

The moisture log.

Dated readings show what was wet, what dried and when the structure met its target. It is also strong support for a claim.

Proof of insurance.

Ask for a certificate if you want one. It shows coverage for the company, not for your home.

For reference, ANSI/IICRC S500 is the published standard for professional water damage restoration, and S520 covers mold remediation. When to call: if water is active or spreading, or if you are unsure whether materials are dry, call the 24/7 line.
