No honest number exists without seeing the damage. These are the factors that move the price and how to compare quotes fairly.

Why there is no single price.

Water damage restoration is priced by what is damaged, how much of it and how contaminated the water is. A small clean spill in one room and a flooded basement with sewage are different jobs, and any figure quoted without seeing the damage is a guess. National cost guides give wide ranges for that reason.

What you can expect is a written scope and a price explained before work starts. That is the standard to hold any company to.

What drives the price.

These factors set the range.

Factor.Why it matters.
Water category.Contaminated water adds protective equipment, disposal and cleaning.
Area affected.More square feet means more pumps, fans and labor.
Materials.Hardwood, carpet, drywall and insulation each react differently.
Time since the loss.Long-wet materials need more removal.
Drying days.Equipment time is a main cost.
Access and hours.Night calls and tight spaces add labor.
Rebuild.Repair is separate from mitigation.

Typical price components.

Most invoices are built from a few parts. There is an emergency service fee for the response, extraction priced by time or area, equipment priced per day, labor for removal and cleaning, antimicrobial treatment and disposal. Repairs are a separate estimate.

Ask whether equipment is billed per piece per day and how many pieces the crew plans to use. A job with ten air movers and two dehumidifiers for five days has a very different total from one with four air movers for three days, and a good scope explains why.

Mitigation and repair are priced separately.

Many invoices have two parts. The first is emergency mitigation: extraction, removal of ruined materials and drying. The second is repair: new drywall, flooring, paint and trim. Some companies do both and some do only the first. Ask for the two to be separated so you can compare.

Insurance may cover one or both parts depending on the cause of the water. See structural drying for what mitigation includes.

How to compare quotes.

Compare apples to apples.

  • Ask for the scope in writing, room by room..
  • Check what equipment is included and for how many days..
  • Ask how drying is verified and whether you get the readings..
  • Ask what happens if more damage is found..
  • Ask what is excluded, such as repairs or contents..

Insurance and out-of-pocket costs.

If the loss is covered, you pay your deductible and the insurer pays covered amounts. If it is not covered, such as flood without a flood policy, you pay the full amount. Ask your insurer before work starts, and keep all invoices and photos.

Be careful of promises that everything will be covered. Only your insurer can decide. The crew supplies documentation. For a flooded basement or storm loss, see basement water damage and storm water damage.

Reducing surprise costs.

Surprises often come from hidden damage found after walls are opened, from extra drying days and from items that turn out to be unsalvageable. Ask what the crew will do if they find more, who approves it and how they will price it.

Keep your own list of questions and answers. A short email confirming what was said gives both sides a record. Honest companies are comfortable with this.

Ways to avoid a bigger bill.

Act fast. Stop the water, call early and start drying. The same loss costs more when materials sit wet for days. Know where your shutoff is, replace old hoses and fix small leaks.

Do not skip drying to save money. Closing up wet walls leads to mold and a second, larger repair.

Small jobs versus big ones.

A small clean-water leak caught within hours might need a single visit, a few fans and a day or two of drying. A flooded basement with contaminated water may need pumps, protective gear, demolition, disposal and weeks of follow-up. The two jobs can differ in price by an order of magnitude.

Do not compare them with each other. Compare quotes for the same loss, with the same scope, from companies that have both seen the damage. If a quote seems far lower than the others, ask what has been left out.

Reading an insurance estimate.

Insurers often produce their own estimates using software and regional price lists. A restoration company's invoice may differ from that estimate. When they do, the usual process is for the company to send supporting documentation, such as photos, logs and a scope, and for the adjuster to review it.

You are the policyholder, and you can ask your insurer to explain any difference. Do not sign anything that you do not understand, and keep copies of every document you receive.

Questions about deductibles.

Your deductible is the amount you pay before insurance applies. A claim for a loss only a little above the deductible may not be worth filing, and claims can affect future premiums. Ask your agent how a claim would be recorded and whether you can discuss options before opening one.

For reference, ANSI/IICRC S500 is the published standard for professional water damage restoration, and S520 covers mold remediation. When to call: if water is active or spreading, or if you are unsure whether materials are dry, call the 24/7 line.
